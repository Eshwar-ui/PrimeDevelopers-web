When you start looking at income-producing commercial property, two terms come up in almost every conversation: the **cap rate** and the **NNN lease**. They're often quoted together, sometimes in the same breath, and they're easy to nod along to without being quite sure what sits behind them.

This guide explains both in plain English — what each one measures, how they fit together, and the questions worth asking before you put capital into a stabilized property.

## What a cap rate actually measures

A capitalization rate — "cap rate" for short — expresses a property's annual net operating income as a percentage of its price.

> Cap rate = Net operating income ÷ Property price

Take a purely illustrative example. A building that produces $150,000 a year in net operating income and is priced at $2,500,000 has a cap rate of 6%. If the same income were available at $2,000,000, the cap rate would be 7.5%.

Put simply, the cap rate tells you how much income a property generates for every dollar of its price, before any financing. It's a snapshot, not a forecast: on its own it says nothing about how that income might change, how long it's secured for, or what the property will be worth later.

## Net operating income: the number everything rests on

A cap rate is only as reliable as the net operating income (NOI) underneath it. NOI is the property's rental income minus the costs of operating it — property taxes, insurance, maintenance and management — **before** mortgage payments and income taxes.

That's why two properties quoted at the same cap rate can be very different investments. Before relying on any cap rate, it's worth understanding:

- Whether the NOI is based on **actual** collected rent or on projected rent
- Which operating costs the owner pays and which the tenants pay
- Whether vacancies, free-rent periods or upcoming lease expirations are reflected
- How long the current leases have left to run

## What "triple-net" means

In a triple-net lease — written **NNN** — the tenant pays its share of the property's three main operating costs on top of its base rent:

1. **Property taxes**
2. **Building insurance**
3. **Common-area maintenance (CAM)** — upkeep of shared spaces such as parking, landscaping and lighting

Under a gross lease, by contrast, the landlord pays those costs out of the rent. Shifting them to the tenant is what makes NNN income comparatively predictable for the owner: if taxes or insurance rise, the tenant carries the increase instead of it coming out of the landlord's return. There's a shorter definition in our [field guide entry on NNN leases](/learn/nnn-lease).

## Why the two are discussed together

Because NNN tenants cover most operating costs, the rent an owner collects sits close to the property's net operating income. That makes the cap rate on a well-leased NNN property easier to read: there are fewer moving parts between the rent roll and the income the cap rate is built on.

It's also why NNN properties are often described as **largely passive**. With taxes, insurance and maintenance handled under the lease, day-to-day involvement for the owner is kept to a minimum. Largely passive isn't the same as hands-off, though — leases still need administering, renewals still need negotiating, and the property still needs looking after.

## A higher cap rate isn't automatically better

It's tempting to read a cap rate like an interest rate and assume higher is better. It isn't that simple. Cap rates largely reflect how the market prices **risk**: a property with an established tenant on a long lease in a proven location will usually trade at a lower cap rate than one with shorter leases or less certain income.

When you compare cap rates, compare the things that drive them:

- **Tenant strength** — the business behind the lease, and how established it is
- **Lease length** — how many years of income are contractually in place
- **Location** — visibility, access and the surrounding trade area
- **Property condition** — what capital spending might be needed down the line
- **Lease terms** — rent escalations, renewal options, and exactly which costs are passed through

A lower cap rate on secure, long-term income can be the more conservative choice. A higher one may be fair compensation for real risk — or a sign of a problem that hasn't been priced in yet.

## Questions to ask before you invest

Whatever the opportunity, these questions will tell you more than the headline cap rate alone:

1. Is the NOI based on signed leases and collected rent, or on projections?
2. Who are the tenants, and how long do their leases run?
3. Is every lease truly triple-net, or are some costs still the owner's?
4. What rent increases are built into the leases, and when do they apply?
5. What capital work — roof, parking, building systems — might the property need in the next several years?
6. How does this cap rate compare with similar properties nearby, and why is it different?

## The bottom line

A cap rate is a useful shorthand for how a property's income relates to its price, and a triple-net lease is one of the clearest ways to make that income predictable. Neither tells the whole story on its own. The leases, the tenants and the property behind the number are what make it worth relying on.
